Merons

According to traditional chemistry, "isotopes" are two or more distinct "atoms" that have the same number of "protons" (atomic number) and a different number of "neutrons". An example of this is "deuterium" and "tritium", with a "proton" and 1.2 neutrons respectively. With different masses and these “atoms” have similar chemical properties.

It also happens that two or more types of "atoms" can have the same number of "neutrons" and different numbers of "protons" and "masses". For example, consider Boron: Z=5 and A=11, with 5 "protons" and 6 "neutrons", and Carbon: Z=6 and A=12, with 6 "protons" and 6 "neutrons". In these cases, these atoms will have different chemical properties, and these are isotones.

They can also be isobars, "atoms" of different chemical elements and, therefore, of different atomic numbers (Z), but which have the same "mass" number (A). Examples include Carbon 6 C 14 (A=14 and Z=6), Nitrogen 7 N 14 (A=14 and Z=7), Argon 18 Ar 40 (A=40 and Z=18) and Calcium 20 Ca 40 (A=40 and Z=20). So that each simple substance is, in fact, a mixture of two or three different types of "atoms", with one of the varieties always in greater proportion to the others. This mixture is proven by instruments such as mass spectrographs.

But, according to TUIDG, it is not logical that the simple addition or removal of a supposed "proton" or "neutron" to a supposed nucleus, in fact, functionally impossible, is capable of influencing the properties of substances in such a drastic way. It is more logical, then, that the particular properties of all types of simple substances are basically due to the sum of the different types of structural forms of the Merons that constitute them, forms that, in turn, are modified by the addition of Cuboctahedra to the pre-existing Meronic structure.

Each simple substance has its global structure more or less compacted as a result of a greater or lesser degree of fit of these different spatial forms, and it is these structural differences that allow greater or lesser distance and mobility of the Merons collectively. This characteristic of fittings means that the Interaction Circles (the lines of force) of each Compalpha of the Merons can be connected in greater or lesser numbers, causing each type of substance to present greater or lesser hardness, malleability, thermal or electrical conductivity, among other characteristics.

Merons with the same number of Cuboctahedra are called Isocubic Merons and Merons with a different number of Cuboctahedra are called Heterocubic Merons. Regarding the availability of distinct structural arrangements for their Cuboctahedra in space, Isocubic Merons can be Isomorphic or Heteromorphic and Heterocubic Merons can only be Heteromorphic. Isomorphic Isocubics (iso-iso) are those that have the same number of Cuboctahedra and the same structural form, therefore developing the same physicochemical function. They are absolutely identical elements, they are 100% pure elements. Heteromorphic Isocubics (iso-hetero), on the other hand, are those that have the same number of cuboctahedra, but a different structural form, therefore developing different physicochemical functions.

According to TUIDG, the different physicochemical characteristics of Merons are closely related, not to the number of Compalphas itself, but to the arrangement or disposition provided by such elements in their constitution. The convenient addition of a single Cuboctahedron to the pre-existing structure of a given Meron completely changes its shape in space and, therefore, its degree of interactivity with the others. If the global structure of a given set of Merons, whether iso-iso, iso-hetero or hetero-hetero, provides more satisfactory fits, provides "stronger" connections between the Interaction Circles than those of any other set of Merons, then this set will, in this specific case of densification, have at least a greater density and/or hardness. By understanding the individual structures of Merons, their specific properties can be obtained. Properties such as internal tension forces, level of density and/or hardness, melting and boiling points, thermal and electrical conductivities, etc., among many other aspects.
