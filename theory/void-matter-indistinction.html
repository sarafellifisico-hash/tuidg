02.1Axiomatic Principles

The Axiomatic Principle of Void–Matter Indistinction

The principle examines the logical impossibility of distinguishing supposed substance from Absolute Empty Space at a fundamental level.

Chapter

Do matter and energy exist as "being"? Do they truly exist as something absolutely distinct from "non-being," distinct from absolute non-existence?

The Axiomatic Principle of Void-Matter Indistinction, proposed by TUIDG, is intrinsic to the supposed "substantial structural elements" of quantum mechanics theory, such as "fermions" (matter particles) and "bosons" (energy particles). It establishes that it would be impossible for there to be any distinction between any type of "substantial structural element" and the Absolute Empty Space that would contain them, and which would necessarily surround them. This Axiomatic Principle is something intrinsic to these "substantial structural elements," for it only exists, only possesses validity, and only makes sense if such "corpuscles" truly existed, dissolved within the Absolute Empty Space that would necessarily contain them.

The Logical Impossibility of Differentiation

This logical principle demonstrates that the truly fundamental problem regarding the supposed existence of substantiality is related solely to the inherent logical impossibility of differentiation between any of the infinite parts of these "substantial structural elements" and the Absolute Empty Space that would contain them. Since that which would constitute a part, and that which would contain it, can only be identical to themselves—and, being unable to be different from themselves, the parts could not be the same as that which would contain them, just as the converse is true. It is logical that from "nothingness," something cannot arise, just as something cannot disappear. Based on these fundamental premises, we can extract two fundamental hypotheses regarding what we understand to be the substance of the universe, though we shall see that only one is absolutely logical.

The first hypothesis is that this supposed substance has "eternal existence." This hypothesis is the precursor to the understanding of the Axiomatic Principle of Logical-Constructive Interactive Incapability and the Axiomatic Principle of Necessarily Indefinite Fragmentation, both of which would render the existence of this supposed substance impossible on two logical fronts. The second hypothesis—the logical hypothesis—is that such substance "eternally does not exist." This hypothesis is the precursor to the understanding that there can only be absolutely immaterial geometric elements as the basis for all supposed "material reality"; therefore, that there exists only an immaterial "geometric reality," and that there exists only an Apparent Reality.

The Problematic of Ideas

The first of these fundamental hypotheses—that is, the eternal existence of substance—in itself makes any attempt to attribute an origin to the universe impossible, such as that described by the standard cosmological model. This model involves the advent of the Big Bang, which possesses, so to speak, a "birth date" of an insignificant 13.7 billion years ago, or any other age that might be attributed to it. By this first fundamental hypothesis, all of cosmological physics would already be removed from the quest for this understanding, since, by this idea of surreal expansion, something solid—something "real"—would have emerged from the unreal, from absolute nothingness. Thus, we may say that this ridiculous and absurd idea of the Big Bang characterizes itself, above all, as a "large carpet" under which all the dirt of deceptive thought and human ignorance is swept.

According to the coherence of TUIDG, the supposition that a real substance exists eternally is far from the definitive answer to what characterizes itself as humanity's primary ontological problem. In truth, the idea that the universe emerged, was born, or came to be from that which was not, is nothing more than a subterfuge required by the apparent impossibility of a definitive solution. This first fundamental hypothesis remains immersed in the same problematic as the eternal existence of a God who supposedly created everything.

The second of the fundamental hypotheses—that is, the eternal non-existence of substance—is far more plausible, for it forces us to admit that space, and what we call "time," exist eternally, without the need for any supposed creation or spontaneous emergence. With this idea in mind, we must admit that there is no fundamental difference between a second and a year, or between a millisecond and a billion years, for any lapse of time in this infinite chain can be infinitely brief if related to an interval suitably larger than it, or infinitely extensive if related to an interval suitably smaller than it.

Even in eternity, if we were to seek the moment of this unusual change, we would never reach the fateful lapse of time where absolute non-existence would pass into absolute existence, for the interval of time in which something would eventually cease to "not-be" and pass to "be" would always remain in an interval of time infinitely prior. But this is, nonetheless, merely the logical realization that there would never be a moment for the supposed "substantialization" of the cosmos in "eternity." According to TUIDG's propositions, the principal ontological problem concerns the differentiation between the supposed substance (matter and energy) and the Absolute Empty Space that would necessarily contain it, and not the unattainable moment of its supposed origin in time; that is: what is the fundamental difference between such "matter," in itself, and Absolute Empty Space, and to what extent can we distinguish between one and the other "element"?

Now, we can observe, in diverse ways, that supposed "matter" has what is called "mass," and therefore has "form" and occupies a place in Absolute Empty Space, whereas this same Absolute Empty Space, by being "empty and infinite," has neither one nor the other distinction. We can also verify that supposed "matter" has, or can be, "energy"; however, "energy" is, in the final analysis, a "discrete quantity" of "mass" which possesses "form" and is in motion throughout Absolute Empty Space. Therefore, "energy" or "mass" (matter) are types of "forms" in motion within Absolute Empty Space, while the latter has none of these attributes.

Although physicists who defend the Standard Cosmological Model, without any philosophical reflection, admit the existence of "massless particles," such as the supposed "photons"—which would not possess "mass" and therefore would not possess "form" within Absolute Empty Space—at a first glance, we can conclude that "forms" are present in all aspects of what we can call "existence," for they define both what we call "mass" or "matter" and what we call "energy." Therefore, it is "forms" that constitute the fundamental difference between supposed "substantiality" and the Absolute Empty Space that contains them.

The Geometric Content of Geometric Forms

If we had any type of substance, it would be constituted by parts that would have "forms"; thus, from a strictly logical and rational point of view, we could divide these "forms" indefinitely, such that we could never arrive at a "form" that could not be divided into other "forms." Thus, what is the absolute content of these forms? What would be their substance? Regarding supposed "matter," how could we arrive at a "form" whose division of content would not result in a new "form" with an even smaller content?

Does the universe have a substantial content? Where? Distributed among its superclusters of galaxies? Within each galaxy? Does a galaxy have content? Where? In its stars? In its planets? But where is the material content of the stars and planets? In their grains of sand, in their molecules, or in their Merons (atoms)?

But where is the material content of the Merons (atoms)? In their supposed "electrons," or in their supposed "quarks"? Then, where is the material content of these supposed "electrons" and "quarks"? Where is the content of that which we call "matter"? Where is that "little particle" whose content cannot result in another "little particle" that has "content" which has no parts?

Do you understand the point I am making? See then: the name I give to this "rational, axiomatic, and incessant search" for the "indivisible content" of the universe’s supposed "matter" is the Axiomatic Principle of Necessarily Indefinite Fragmentation. This principle only has value—it only makes sense—if "matter particles" actually existed.

Observe that there is no "matter" between one galaxy and another, beyond gas molecules and dust. That there is no "matter" between one star and another, beyond gas molecules and dust. That there is no "matter" between one planet and another, beyond gas molecules and dust. That there is no "matter" between one grain of dust and another, beyond gas molecules and the dust itself. That there is no "matter" between one gas molecule and another, or between one Meron (atom) and another, beyond the gas molecules and the Merons (atoms) themselves.

However, if Merons (atoms) were truly constituted by "quarks" and "electrons," then, by the logic of the continuity of indefinite divisibility, there would also be no "matter" between these supposed "particles," except within the supposed "quarks" and "electrons" themselves. And thus, by numerical extrapolation of this same logic of indefinite divisibility, we can also suppose that no "matter" should exist between the "elements" that would necessarily constitute these supposed "little particles," just as there would be none between the "elements" that would necessarily constitute those, and so on, ad infinitum. Where, then, is the "matter"?

What we must understand is that the supposed "matter" does not get "stuck" in the "virtual layers" of these supposed "material structures," as if it were honey being transferred from one container to another, sticking, little by little, to the walls of these successive recipients. That which we call "matter" is merely "forms" without "materiality," filling "forms" without "materiality." There is no honey. Thus, it makes no sense to say something like "inside the quarks" or "inside" the "protons," as if we were saying "the honey inside the jar," for, in these cases, there are no "walls," "shells," or "casings" that would constitute such a "honey jar"—which, in the case of supposed "particles," would be the "casings" containing their supposed constituent elements. If "quarks" existed forming "protons," then there would be no "inside of the protons"; there would only be "quarks."
