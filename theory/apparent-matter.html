The Apparent Matter of the Universe

Apparent Matter is not substantial materiality, but the functional geometry of Compalphas organized within Absolute Empty Space.

Apparent Matter

When considering philosophical and physical conceptions of substance and matter, it is certain that, due to their unreal characteristics, we shall also arrive at inconsistencies. The early philosophers did not speak specifically of space or matter; rather, they sought the Arkhé, that is, the absolute principle of all that exists. For them, the Arkhé would be that which comes before and stands prior to all, at the beginning and at the end of all things, at the foundation; it would be the immortal, immutable, and incorruptible background of all things, which brought them into existence and which governs them. It is the origin, not as something left in the past, but as that which, here and now, gives rise to everything; it is perennial and permanent.

The Attributes of Apparent Matter

According to TUGIA, we shall see that these eternal and immutable qualities, which would be intrinsic to everything we understand as materiality, substantiality, or physical reality, are perfectly applicable to the structure of the functional geometric elements, referred to here as Alpha Components or Compalphas. These are the immaterial geometric elements constituting the Geometric Continua that form the Geometric Hypercontinuum, where Apparent Reality manifests. Conversely, for modern physics, matter is, broadly speaking, defined as everything that occupies a place in space, being constituted by corpuscles with an insurmountable minimum dimension, and quantified in absolute value. Its totality occupies space with a maximum dimension that is also insurmountable; that is, its totality is invariable, possesses a structure that cannot be reduced, and occupies practically the same volume as that supposed quantified space attributed to the known universe.

According to TUGIA, the apparent materiality of the cosmos is not due to the existence of contradictory, massively fundamental particles, but is due to the interactions of specific types of geometric elements organized in different positions and structural dimensions within Absolute Empty Space. Matter, therefore, is merely an appearance that becomes a palpable reality due to infinite possibilities of interaction of functional and dimensionless geometric elements termed, in the context of this theory, Compalphas. These are fundamental elements—fundamental, however, only in terms of functionality, not in dimensionality, as they exist in infinite dimensions of their own.

Apparent Matter is the second fundamental element of the universe and possesses the attributes given by the following five postulates:

I. Apparent Matter is an Immaterial geometry, for it is impossible to arrive, in any way, at truly solid and fundamental structures, since the restrictive principles of the Void-Matter Indistinction, the Necessarily Indefinite Fragmentation, and the Logical-Constructive Interactive Incapability, respectively, would make it impossible for something to emerge from nothing, for it to remain in a stable base dimension, and for it to possess logical functionality in such a supposed reality.

II. Apparent Matter is a functional geometry, for it is governed by two geometric mechanisms that exist in two types of distinct geometric structures called the Geometric Continuum and the Geometric Hypercontinuum. Such mechanisms are the Rotational Interaction, responsible for the formation and stabilization of the structure of the Geometric Continuum, and the Cyclic Interaction, responsible for remote communication between the parts of the elements (of the bodies) in the Geometric Hypercontinuum.

III. Apparent Matter is a dimensionless geometry, for the individual geometric elements constituting the Geometric Continua—in this case, the Compalphas—can have any dimension, since they are disposed within the infinite Absolute Empty Space, that is, in the Omega Dimension of TUGIA. Thus, from any dimension of Compalphas, there can exist infinite dimensions of Geometric Continua, therefore, of Geometric Hypercontinua, and consequently, infinite base dimensions for infinite apparent realities. Each type of simple or composite form present in the structure of the Geometric Hypercontinuum possesses an infinity of copies at infinite points in the space of this geometric mesh, each with an infinity of dimensions.

IV. Apparent Matter is an infinitely dense geometry, for besides the possibility of the existence of infinite Geometric Continua of the same structural dimension, superimposed at the same points in space and at infinite other points, they can also be rotated at infinite angles from each of the infinite points. Each type of simple or composite form present in the structure of the Geometric Hypercontinuum possesses an infinity of copies at infinite points in the space of this geometric mesh.

V. Apparent Matter is tri-directed, for it is not the Absolute Empty Space that has this property, but the geometric elements present within it. Each type of elementary form, with the exception of the point, has its own set of orientations in space.

Space Dimensions or Directions in Space?

In TUGIA, the Structural Dimension of elements refers exclusively to the size of such elements in space; that is, it refers to how much a certain form—such as a line, a plane, or a sphere—is larger than any other form. This implies that there can exist infinite Structural Dimensions for any existing geometric element and, by extension, for any body immersed in this Apparent Reality. Conversely, the spatial orientation of elements (direction and sense) refers to the capacity for evolution of the forms of such elements (geometric or "material"). That is, it determines in which directions each element can grow, can increase its Structural Dimensions, and in which directions they can move (in an apparent manner) within this space. Although it may seem that there are infinite spatial orientations, in reality, there are only, let us say, six pure orientations—that is, orientations that are not a composition of more than one of the three directions with three perpendicular senses of space.

For example, from any point P, any extension simply "To the Right" (Pd) and "To the Left" (Pe) constitutes two orientations of space; they form a line, which is only one (arbitrary) direction with a double sense starting from P. Likewise, simply "Upward" (Pc) and "Downward" (Pb), or "Forward" (Pf) and "Backward" (Pt), are also a single direction with a double sense, respectively. All infinite spatial orientations, other than these six pure ones, are compositions of these. For instance, in relation to this "." (a point on the monitor or sheet of paper), which we may call point A, and in relation to my point of view, which we may call point B, another point situated at half the distance (between A and B) at a height given by $h = \frac{l\sqrt{3}}{2}$, called point C, when connected by lines, culminates in the formation of a flat figure named an equilateral triangle.

Thus, a plane, for example, is a fundamentally bi-directed structure, for it can only increase or decrease (evolve) the Structural Dimension (size) of its form (arbitrary Structural Dimension) along two directions or four senses, starting from any point on its surface. This is because, from any point on this surface, only two mutually perpendicular directions, each with two senses opposite to each other, evolve in space. In a plane with such a property, we can evolve, for example, two circumferences of a common center, and these can have relative Structural Dimensions (sizes) that can be infinitely opposed (even if the plane is not infinite), since the area of the plane can be infinitely fractionated into smaller parts. Similarly, a cube or a sphere, for example, are fundamentally tri-directional elements, for they can undergo increases (evolve) in the Structural Dimensions (in the sizes) of their respective forms along three directions or six senses, starting from any point in their interior, as from any of these points, only three directions evolve in space, all perpendicular to one another, each with two senses that are opposite to each other. In geometric solids such as these, we can also evolve (increase) any other geometric elements contained within them with infinitely opposed relative Structural Dimensions, since there exists a capacity for indefinite fragmentation for geometric elements in Absolute Empty Space.

What conventional physicists commonly say about space itself is that it may have a certain number of Structural Dimensions, when they should be saying DIRECTIONS, so as not to confuse them with possible sizes of space. In string theory and its variants, for example, one speaks of absurdities such as 10, 11, 23... Structural Dimensions for space. They claim that the extra Structural Dimensions (extra directions) of space cannot be seen or detected by instruments because they are compacted or curled upon themselves and are incredibly small—smaller than the Planck length—as if space itself were something similar to loops made in the vibrating strings of that theory's context, or as if directions could be curled to create new directions. In TUGIA, space, being an Absolute Void, cannot have attributes of form, for if it were like a sphere of infinite radius, it would be contradictory. An infinite sphere would not have a quantified radius and, therefore, would not be spherical, since a sphere is the product of surfaces equidistant from a center. Furthermore, it would still be a spherical "something" within the Absolute Void.

By possessing no center or surface to delimit its form, such an element possesses infinite directions and senses, originating from infinite points within its infinite volume. For one of the infinite points that lies at the center of gravity (barycenter) of a geometric solid, then, in relation to this point, the solid is structured along six spatial senses (three directions $\times$ two senses). There exist, in Absolute Empty Space, infinite such hexa-oriented sets, one for each geometric element, formed by three perpendicular directions that generate six distinct senses—that is, six half-lines originating from a single point at the center of the elements in question. Since each of the spatial senses possesses infinite extension, we can take one of them as an absolute reference to quantify the apparent movements of all universal structures. For example, since the Earth emerged probably 4.5 billion years ago, its current and absolute spatial position (relative to such an absolute reference) could, in principle, be measured (had the origin of the event been marked at that moment). Its position is truly measurable, although we cannot measure it due to a lack of adequate technological means and due to universal structures being attracted in many different directions by gravitational effects of superior structures.

As an example of the application of these concepts of spatial orientations, a line of Immaterial Geometry can only be represented in a bi-directed space—that is, with two directional components, such as Pci-Pba, Pdi-Pes, Pfe-Pta, and so on. A plane can only be represented in a quadri-oriented form—that is, with four directional components, such as Pdi-Pes-Pfe-Pta or any combination of only four of the six pure orientations. A geometric solid, however, can only be represented in a Hexa-oriented form (6S)—that is, with six directional components, such as the six distinct senses. Thus, all existing elements, save for points, lines, and planes, are geometric solids being represented in Hexa-oriented form.
