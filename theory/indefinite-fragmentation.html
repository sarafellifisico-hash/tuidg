The Axiomatic Principle of Necessarily Indefinite Fragmentation

Any supposed substantial element with quantified dimensions would remain divisible into further structural parts indefinitely.

Chapter

The Axiomatic Principle of Necessarily Indefinite Fragmentation, proposed by TUIDG, is intrinsic to supposed Substantial Structural Elements (matter or energy), as it establishes that it is impossible for any type of Substantial Structural Element with a quantified Structural Dimension to exist that cannot be divided. Consequently, this Axiomatic Principle would only possess validity and would only make sense if such Substantial Structural Elements truly existed, dissolved within the Absolute Empty Space that would necessarily contain them—as is the case with the supposed "material corpuscles" of conventional physical description.

This logical, axiomatic principle demonstrates that the truly fundamental problem regarding the supposed existence of substantiality is related solely to the logical impossibility of the non-divisibility of these supposed Substantial Structural Elements. Consequently, this infinite divisibility of Structural Elements runs counter to the very existence of the supposed substance of being, since an elementary and indivisible unit of anything is impossible. Therefore, for any supposedly substantial Structural Element of any Structural Dimension that might exist, its half—or any other proportion—would necessarily be required.

The Problematic of Ideas

Physicists commonly assert that the most basic supposed "material particles" (fermions), such as quarks and electrons, are like mathematical points—that is, devoid of any structure—despite claiming that they possess mass, energy, momentum, and interact mutually. Yet, they claim these particles do not interact by themselves, but through supposed even more fundamental "energy particles" (bosons) created or generated by them, such as photons and gluons. Thus, we can see that the problem with physicists is their failure to admit that such concepts and premises are essentially contradictory and inconsistent; for something that lacks spatial structure cannot "create" other structures to promote its own interaction!

If the supposed Substantial Structural Elements generated at the singularity (the Big Bang) were corpuscular, absolutely nothing would prevent their totality from being distributed equally and extremely uniformly throughout the space permitted to them, in parts whose sizes would be, relative to one another, infinitely large and infinitely small—and therefore, infinite in number. We would thus have a formal numerical infinity, represented by these "particles," within a formal numerical finitude, which would be the finite space that emerged.

However, the problem is that if we admit the existence of many parts rather than just one, we must accept that each part possesses a beginning, a middle, and an end if it is extended, and an "inside" and an "outside" if it is extended and voluminous. For if, from the beginning of any part to its midpoint, it is necessary that there be another half or middle term to complete it—otherwise, there would be a discontinuity—then between this new half and that same beginning, there must also be another half, then another, and yet another. Therefore, a part possesses infinite parts, each with its own necessary infinite divisibility.

This is not to say that this or that eventual part has its own volume or extension divided indefinitely as a function of this property of necessarily indefinite fragmentation; rather, it implies that other parts of the same nature possess their own Structural Dimensions that are indefinitely smaller in relation to this or that specific part, since each of them, separately, would not possess other parts (which is impossible) to constitute them. Consequently, we should not concern ourselves with the mode of this eventual divisibility, for there would be no difference whatsoever between a purely logical, rational divisibility and an eventual physical divisibility that might occur, given that we accept the existence of many parts rather than just one. Therefore, whatever the mode by which the universe might have managed to divide the supposed material parts that would have to exist, that same mode would have to continue the process of indefinite division.

Thus, in a universe such as this, if it were possible, we would have (or observe) various types of malformed elements of all sizes, distributed uniformly and dispersively throughout empty space, colliding randomly as a result of the frantic motion described by other elements of the same nature, driven by the energy of the supposed Big Bang, in a chaotic scenario lacking any hierarchy of structural interactivity and completely different from the functional universe we know.

Even if the supposed structural elements could not be divided indefinitely—that is, if the fundamental restrictive property of Necessarily Indefinite Fragmentation were to lose all validity—there would still remain two incapacitating problems of the same magnitude that would render the logical constitution of the universe impossible: the problem of Logical-Constructive Interactive Incapability and the problem of Void-Matter Indistinction. According to the propositions of TUIDG, the Axiomatic Principle of Necessarily Indefinite Fragmentation and the Axiomatic Principle of Necessarily Indefinite Containment are equivalent, yet absolutely opposite in dimension; that is, the former applies to the supposed Substantial Structural Elements that form the microcosm (the "fundamental particles" of physicists), while the latter applies to the structural elements of the macrocosm—to the very "volume" of the supposed universe emerged from nothing. Therefore, they function as Relative Restrictive Axiomatic Principles.
