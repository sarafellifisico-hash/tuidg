Dimension, Direction, and Level of Organization of Cosmic Elements

Regarding the etymological origin of the word "Dimension"—derived from the Latin "dimensio" or "dimetri", a compound of "dis-" ("out" or "apart") and "metri" ("to measure")—it signifies an "external measure" or the measurement of bodies and solids. Since physicists use two different terms for the same characteristic of space or the elements (bodies) contained within it—terms considered imprecise—TUIDG proposes fundamental distinctions between the concepts of "dimension" and "direction," as well as the introduction of a specific parameter: the "Level of Structural Organization."

In this theory, the concept of "Dimension" refers exclusively to the "size" of bodies in space or the "size" of a specific "portion" of space itself—for measurement purposes, such as a cubic meter of space. Conversely, the concept of "direction" refers exclusively to the directions in which bodies can be oriented or move from a given point within that space. For instance, consider a cube or a sphere: these elements are structured along "three spatial directions" and are thus "Tri-directional (3D)" formal elements. A plane, having only two directions, is a "Bi-directional (2D)" formal element; a line is a "Uni-directional (1D)" formal element; and a point is an "A-directional (0D)" element—and therefore "A-dimensional"—meaning it possesses neither direction (0D) nor size.

The "Level of Structural Organization," in turn, refers exclusively to a set of distinct elements—possessing various possible "dimensions" and "sizes"—that interact with one another within a functional and structural hierarchy. Thus, each type of assembly—ranging from Merons, molecules, molecular compounds, dust grains, stones, planets, stars, and galaxies to all bodies possessing their own Structural Dimensions—constitutes a specific type of organization for its spatial structures. Within TUIDG, the specific size reference for each cosmic structure is defined by the term "Structural Dimension"; consequently, there exist infinite "Structural Dimensions" within a universe that also possesses an infinite Spatial Dimension—referred to here as the Omega Dimension.

Because we consistently encounter sets of entities with varying Structural Dimensions participating in specific functional systems—such as stellar systems, which are composed of elements ranging in size from Merons to the star itself, following a dimensional and functional hierarchy rather than a uniform distribution of Merons, stars, and galaxies—we must consider how many distinct levels of organization these structures occupy to form the universe as a whole. Or, more precisely, we must consider how many distinct "Levels of Structural Organization" we are capable of identifying—observing either directly or indirectly—within the universe; for in reality, according to TUIDG, there exist infinite "Coexisting Levels of Structural Organization" that lie beyond any logical possibility of direct or indirect observation. We cannot "empirically" prove that the universe is spatially infinite; we can only prove it axiomatically, through reason.

Thus, to define how many "Structural Levels of Organization" exist for the elements of the universe within this theory, we can categorize any element that—when performing a collective action or an autonomous, distinct collective function—differs completely, in its totality, from the elements constituting its own global structure. Consider, for instance, a star: the totality of the Merons (atoms) constituting it possesses a structure and functionality completely different from those of the individual Merons (atoms) themselves. The same applies to a galaxy: its overall structure and functionality differ entirely from the structure and functionality of the individual stars and gas clouds—the constituent parts—that make it up. We can therefore begin to define "Structural Levels of Organization" based on the types of elements that constitute each of these universal structures, regardless of their specific structural dimensions; this allows us to group such elements into a "Dimensional Range," with each "Structural Level of Organization" possessing its own "Dimensional Range"—sometimes narrower, sometimes broader.
